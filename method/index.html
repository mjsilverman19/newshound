Method

Abstract

Newshound is an automated news service that uses prediction markets as a detector for news. Each hour it compares the prices of roughly 55,000 Polymarket and Kalshi markets with their prices an hour earlier, flags markets that moved at least 5 percentage points with at least $1,000 traded during the move, groups flagged markets into stories, and scores each story by the money-weighted size of the shift on a logarithmic scale from 0 to 100. Stories scoring 25 or more are investigated by a language model (Claude, made by Anthropic) using web search and page retrieval. The model proposes an account of what happened and the sources for it; deterministic code, not the model, decides whether the account is published. Publication requires a source that the model's own searches returned, that is not on a list of aggregators and partisan sites, and that was published no later than 15 minutes after the shift began. Stories without such a source are kept as open questions and searched again after 1, 6 and 24 hours. Stories are written and published without human review. Market prices are used only to find stories and never appear in them.

1. Signal

Markets are eligible if they have at least $5,000 of order-book liquidity (Polymarket) or 1,000 contracts of open interest (Kalshi), do not resolve within 12 hours, and are not in categories that track a public feed or a live event, such as weather, crypto and asset prices, and games in progress. Markets about the economy (interest rates, recessions, inflation, and stock-index or oil levels) are the exception: they are kept when they resolve at least 7 days out, because their prices move on news rather than tracking a live feed. A market is flagged when its price, read as a probability, differs by at least 5 points from the previous hourly reading (for Polymarket, also from the platform's own one-hour change). The start of the shift is estimated as the last time the price sat at its earlier level before covering a quarter of the move, using the market's recent price and trade history where available. Flagged moves with less than $1,000 traded since that start are discarded. A market is not flagged again for 6 hours unless it moves a further 5 points.

2. Scoring

For each flagged market m, let Δpm be the absolute price change (0 to 1), Vm the dollars traded since the shift began, and rm the ratio of that trading to the market's usual pace over the same length of time (from its one-week volume on Polymarket and its 24-hour volume on Kalshi). The intensity of the move and the score of a story s are

Im = Δpm · Vm · √min(max(rm, 1), 100)

S = min(100, 10 · log10(1 + Σm∈s Im))

Each 10 points of S is a tenfold increase in money-weighted repricing. For example, a 17-point move on $20,000 of trading at ten times the usual pace scores about 40. Stories scoring 25 or more are investigated; smaller ones are listed under a short topic name. Published stories carry a label for the size of the shift:

  • Extraordinary shift: S ≥ 50
  • Major shift: S ≥ 38
  • Large shift: S ≥ 34
  • Notable shift: S ≥ 30
  • Moderate shift: S ≥ 25

3. Stories

A flagged market joins a story already holding that market, or another market from the same event on the same platform, on the same day (US Eastern time). Otherwise it starts a new story. Stories on the same day that share a distinctive name (a person, organisation or place, ignoring words common to many stories) are then merged, so the same news told through different markets appears once. During investigation the model may also merge stories it finds to be the same news; merged stories are rescored.

On the front page, stories with at least $10,000 traded across their markets come first, each group ordered by score. A thinly traded market can score highly on a small, sudden move, so it can be published but cannot lead the page.

4. Investigation

The model (claude-opus-5-5) receives the shift: which markets moved, in which direction, when, how much was traded, and how each market resolves. It is instructed to infer what kind of development would produce the shift and to search for it, preferring primary sources (statements, filings, dockets, official pages, posts by the people involved) and wire services and established outlets. It may run up to 13 web searches and 10 page retrievals per story (5 searches on a recheck). It records each source's publication time as shown on the page, or none if no time is shown, and returns a headline, summary, dateline, sources with their roles, and a lead image taken from a source's own page metadata. Topic names for smaller stories are written by claude-haiku-5-5.

5. Verification and status

For each source the model marks as the cause of a shift, code checks that (a) its URL was returned by the model's own search or retrieval tools in that run, (b) its site is not one of 34 listed aggregators, re-posters and partisan blogs, which may appear only as context, and (c) its publication time is no later than 15 minutes after the shift began. A date with no time counts as before the shift only if it is a full day earlier. A story is reported if some source meets all three conditions, and timing unclear if the only qualifying sources lack a usable publication time. Both are published. Otherwise the story is on the trail: it is shown as an open question, written so as not to speculate about a cause, and searched again after 1, 6 and 24 hours. If a later search finds a qualifying report published after the shift, the story is published with the delay between the shift and the first report.

6. Limitations

  • Coverage. Only news that people bet on can be detected. Politics, sports and economics are overrepresented; news with no market behind it is invisible.
  • Surprise, not importance. The score measures how far expectations moved in the markets that exist, weighted by the money behind the move. Important news that was widely expected moves prices little and scores low; a surprise in a heavily traded market scores high whatever its wider significance.
  • Timing. Prices are compared an hour apart. Where a market's trade history is sparse, the start of a shift, and therefore the delay before the first report, is accurate only to about an hour.
  • Publication times are read from the pages by the model and can be wrong. Each story lists its sources with their times so readers can check.
  • Model error. The model can misread a source, overstate what it says, or attach a real source to the wrong development. The checks in section 5 limit invented sources and late reports but do not verify that a source says what the summary says.
  • No human review. No person reads stories before they are published.
  • Source lists of aggregators and partisan sites are maintained by hand and are incomplete.

7. Reproducibility and corrections

The code, including every threshold above, is public at github.com/mjsilverman19/newshound; the numbers on this page are read from its configuration when the site is built. To report an error in a story, open an issue there with the story's link.
