Method

Abstract

Newshound is an automated news service that ranks the day's news by how much it changed expectations. Each hour it compares the prices of tens of thousands of Polymarket and Kalshi markets with their prices 24 hours earlier, weights each market's change by how much money normally trades in it, groups markets about the same subject into topics, and scores each topic on a logarithmic scale from 0 to 100. Topics scoring 33 or more are investigated by a language model (Claude, made by Anthropic) using web search and page retrieval, starting from a description of the whole topic: which markets moved and which way, how strong the signal is, and when during the day it moved. The model proposes an account of what happened and the sources for it; deterministic code, not the model, decides whether the account is published. Publication requires a source that the model's own searches returned, that is not on a list of aggregators and partisan sites, and that is from the day of the shift. Topics without such a source are kept as open questions and searched again after 1, 6 and 24 hours. Stories are written and published without human review. Market prices are used only to find and rank stories and never appear in them.

1. Signal

A market counts toward the day's news if its price, read as a probability, has moved at least 3 points over the past 24 hours with at least $500 traded in that time, and it does not resolve within 24 hours. Markets that track a public feed or a live event (weather, crypto and asset prices, games in progress) are left out, except markets about the economy (interest rates, recessions, inflation, and stock-index or oil levels) that resolve at least 7 days out. Sports and entertainment markets are set aside as soft news, and markets settling a finished vote count are left out.

2. Scoring

For each market m, let Δpm be the absolute change in its price over 24 hours (0 to 1) and Nm its normal daily trading in dollars: a week's trading divided by seven on Polymarket, and the last 24 hours' trading on Kalshi, which reports no weekly figure. The day score of a market and the score of a topic t are

Dm = Δpm · Nm

S = min(100, 10 · log10(1 + Σm∈t Dm))

The net change counts, not how much a price swung in between, so a rumour that rose and fell back adds little. Weighting by normal trading means a move in a market where a lot of money usually trades counts for more than the same move in a thin one. Each 10 points of S is a tenfold increase. Topics scoring 33 or more are investigated; topics between 30 and 33 are listed under a short topic name. Each topic also gets a signal strength: strong with a score of at least 37 and at least $40,000 of normal daily trading, weak with under $10,000, or a single small market, and moderate otherwise. A weak topic gets at most 3 searches. Published stories carry a label for the size of the shift:

  • Extraordinary shift: S ≥ 50
  • Major shift: S ≥ 38
  • Large shift: S ≥ 34
  • Notable shift: S ≥ 30
  • Moderate shift: S ≥ 25

3. Stories

Markets in the same event form one group. Groups join a topic when they share two specific tags (tags on at most 6% of the day's moving groups), or one specific tag and a name, or a name in both group titles; a name counts only if at most 4 groups share it, so "Hegseth" ties groups together and "Israel" does not. Each topic becomes a story, or updates the story it already is when they share a group; a topic touching several of the day's stories merges them. During investigation the model may also merge stories it finds to have the same cause, such as a market rout that moved both a company's listing plans and interest-rate expectations; merged stories are rescored. A published story is investigated again only if its score rises by 10 or more.

On the front page, stories with at least $10,000 of normal daily trading across their markets come first, each group ordered by score, so a thin market cannot lead the page.

4. Investigation

The model (claude-opus-5-5) receives the topic: which markets moved over the day and in which direction, how much money normally trades in them, how strong the signal is, when the lead market moved, and how the biggest market resolves. It is instructed to infer what kind of development would produce the shift and to search for it, preferring primary sources (statements, filings, dockets, official pages, posts by the people involved) and wire services and established outlets. It may run up to 13 web searches and 10 page retrievals per story (5 searches on a recheck). It records each source's publication time as shown on the page, or none if no time is shown, and returns a headline, summary, dateline, sources with their roles, and a lead image taken from a source's own page metadata. Topic names for smaller stories are written by claude-haiku-5-5.

5. Verification and status

For each source the model marks as the cause of a shift, code checks that (a) its URL was returned by the model's own search or retrieval tools in that run, (b) its site is not one of 34 listed aggregators, re-posters and partisan blogs, which may appear only as context, and (c) it was published no more than 36 hours before the 24-hour window began; an older report is treated as background, because it cannot be what changed expectations that day. A story is reported if some source meets all three conditions, and timing unclear if the only qualifying sources lack a usable publication time. Both are published. Otherwise the story is on the trail: it is shown as an open question, written so as not to speculate about a cause, and searched again after 1, 6 and 24 hours. If a later search finds a qualifying report, the story is published.

6. Limitations

  • Coverage. Only news that people bet on can be detected. Politics, sports and economics are overrepresented; news with no market behind it is invisible.
  • Surprise, not importance. The score measures how far expectations moved in the markets that exist, weighted by the money behind the move. Important news that was widely expected moves prices little and scores low; a surprise in a heavily traded market scores high whatever its wider significance.
  • Timing. Prices are compared an hour apart. Where a market's trade history is sparse, the start of a shift, and therefore the delay before the first report, is accurate only to about an hour.
  • Publication times are read from the pages by the model and can be wrong. Each story lists its sources with their times so readers can check.
  • Model error. The model can misread a source, overstate what it says, or attach a real source to the wrong development. The checks in section 5 limit invented sources and late reports but do not verify that a source says what the summary says.
  • No human review. No person reads stories before they are published.
  • Source lists of aggregators and partisan sites are maintained by hand and are incomplete.

7. Reproducibility and corrections

The code, including every threshold above, is public at github.com/mjsilverman19/newshound; the numbers on this page are read from its configuration when the site is built. To report an error in a story, open an issue there with the story's link.
